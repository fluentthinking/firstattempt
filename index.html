Reading Passage 1: The Fading Night

If humanity naturally thrived under the moon, the division between day and night would be irrelevant to our species. However, as daytime creatures, we have artificially illuminated the night to accommodate our modern routines. This environmental modification causes light pollution, primarily driven by inefficient lighting designs that cast illumination upward into the atmosphere instead of focusing it downward. This widespread glare affects the natural behaviors—such as migration and reproduction—of countless species.

Historically, nocturnal light pollution was non-existent. Before gaslights, a major city like 19th-century London relied purely on candles. Today, vast regions are saturated in artificial brightness. This profoundly impacts wildlife. Disoriented by city lights, migratory birds often collide with illuminated structures, with younger birds suffering the highest casualty rates. In coastal areas, nesting sea turtles struggle to locate darkened shores, while artificial lights disorient newly hatched turtles trying to reach the ocean. Similarly, amphibians residing near busy roads face severe disruptions to their breeding routines due to extreme artificial glare.

Reading Passage 3: Have teenagers always existed?

Our ancestor, Homo erectus, may not have had culture or even language, but did they have teenagers? That question has been contested in the past few years, with some anthropologists claiming evidence of an adolescent phase in human fossils. This is not merely an academic debate. Humans today are the only animals on Earth to have a teenage phase, yet we have very little idea why. Establishing exactly when adolescence first evolved could help us understand its practical purpose today.

Until recently, the dominant explanation was that physical growth is delayed by our need to grow large brains and to learn all the behaviour patterns associated with humanity. While such behaviour is still developing, humans cannot easily fend for themselves, so it is best to stay small and look youthful. That way your parents and other members of the social group are motivated to continue looking after you.

The human fossil record is extremely sparse, and the number of fossilised children minuscule. Nevertheless, anthropologists have begun to look at what can be learned of the lives of our ancestors from these youngsters. One of the most studied is the famous Turkana boy, an almost complete skeleton of Homo erectus from 1.6 million years ago. Accurately assessing how old someone is from their skeleton is a tricky business, relying on estimates of teeth, bones, and general size.

Susan Anton points to research by Margaret Clegg, who studied 18th and 19th-century skeletons whose ages at death were known. When she tried to age the skeletons without checking the records, she found inconsistencies similar to those of the Turkana boy. Anton thinks that Homo erectus had already developed modern human patterns of growth, with a late adolescent spurt. She believes the Turkana boy was just about to enter it.

Anthropologist Steven Leigh goes further. He believes the idea of adolescence as catch-up growth does not explain why the growth rate increases so dramatically. He says that many apes have growth spurts in particular body regions to coincide with the seasons when food is plentiful, minimizing the risk of lacking food while growing. According to his theory, adolescence evolved as an integral part of efficient upright locomotion, as well as to accommodate more complex brains.

Another approach relies on the minute analysis of tooth growth. Every nine days or so the growing teeth of both apes and humans acquire ridges on their enamel surface. These are like rings in a tree trunk: the number of them tells you how long the crown of a tooth took to form. Across mammals, the rate at which teeth develop is closely related to how fast the brain grows and the age you mature.

A more decisive piece of evidence came last year when researchers published findings from a study of Neanderthal teeth. Neanderthals had much faster tooth growth than Homo erectus. Lead researcher Fernando Ramirez-Rozzi thinks Neanderthals died young primarily because of the cold, harsh environment they had to endure in glacial Europe. They evolved to grow up quicker than their immediate ancestors, probably reaching adulthood quickly without delaying for an adolescent growth spurt.
