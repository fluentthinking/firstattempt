Our Vanishing Night

If humanity naturally thrived under the moon, the division between day and night would be irrelevant to our species. However, as daytime creatures, we have artificially illuminated the night to accommodate our modern routines. This environmental modification causes light pollution, primarily driven by inefficient lighting designs that cast illumination upward into the atmosphere instead of focusing it downward. This widespread glare affects the natural behaviors—such as migration and reproduction—of countless species.

Historically, nocturnal light pollution was non-existent. Before gaslights, a major city like 19th-century London relied purely on candles. Today, vast regions are saturated in artificial brightness. This profoundly impacts wildlife. Disoriented by city lights, migratory birds often collide with illuminated structures, with younger birds suffering the highest casualty rates.

In coastal areas, nesting sea turtles struggle to locate darkened shores, while artificial lights disorient newly hatched turtles trying to reach the ocean. Similarly, amphibians residing near busy roads face severe disruptions to their breeding routines due to extreme artificial glare.

Is there a psychologist in the building?

A. The spaces we inhabit profoundly influence our behavior. While environmental psychologists have vast research on this, many academics have historically maintained an unhelpful attitude, refusing to share findings with practical building planners, preferring to keep their work strictly theoretical.

B. Some studies explicitly compare similar environments. For instance, research analyzing police data demonstrated that residential apartment blocks surrounded by nature and greenery experienced noticeably lower crime rates compared to identical buildings lacking vegetation.

C. Environmental psychologists are increasingly in demand. Professor David Uzzell points to the rebuilding of one south London school as a striking example of how building design can affect human behavior positively.

Ultimately, the fundamental layout of a medical building is the most critical factor in helping patients orient themselves, rather than just the placement of signs.

Have teenagers always existed?

Our ancestor, Homo erectus, may not have had culture or even language, but did they have teenagers? That question has been contested in the past few years, with some anthropologists claiming evidence of an adolescent phase in human fossils. This is not merely an academic debate. Humans today are the only animals on Earth to have a teenage phase, yet we have very little idea why.

Until recently, the dominant explanation was that physical growth is delayed by our need to grow large brains and to learn all the behaviour patterns associated with humanity.

Susan Anton points to research by Margaret Clegg, who studied 18th and 19th-century skeletons whose ages at death were known. When she tried to age the skeletons without checking the records, she found inconsistencies similar to those of the Turkana boy. Anton thinks that Homo erectus had already developed modern human patterns of growth, with a late adolescent spurt.

Anthropologist Steven Leigh goes further. He believes the idea of adolescence as catch-up growth does not explain why the growth rate increases so dramatically. He says that many apes have growth spurts in particular body regions to coincide with the seasons when food is plentiful. According to his theory, adolescence evolved as an integral part of efficient upright locomotion, as well as to accommodate more complex brains.

A more decisive piece of evidence came last year when researchers published findings from a study of Neanderthal teeth. Lead researcher Fernando Ramirez-Rozzi thinks Neanderthals died young primarily because of the cold, harsh environment they had to endure in glacial Europe. They evolved to grow up quicker than their immediate ancestors, probably reaching adulthood quickly without delaying for an adolescent growth spurt.
